At 11:59 on the morning of January 20th, you are a private citizen. At noon, you are in charge of the most powerful military on Earth, a budget of several trillion dollars, and a briefcase that never leaves your side. All it takes is thirty-five words.

This is what it's actually like to be President of the United States. Not the speeches. The days. What you eat, who follows you, what you're allowed to do, and what you'll never do again.

Let's start with getting the job. The Constitution asks for very little. You have to be at least thirty-five years old. You have to be a natural-born citizen. And you have to have lived in the country for fourteen years. That's the whole list. No degree, no exam, no experience required.

Winning is the hard part. Campaigns now run for about two years and cost billions of dollars. You'll shake hands in diner parking lots in Iowa, eat fried food on a stick at state fairs, and repeat the same speech so many times you start dreaming it. Then, on a Tuesday in November, about a hundred and fifty million strangers decide whether any of that worked.

Say it did. Now comes inauguration day.

You put your hand on a Bible, or any book you choose, and say the oath. "I do solemnly swear that I will faithfully execute the Office of President of the United States." Thirty-five words. While you're standing on the steps of the Capitol, a crew of movers races through the White House. In about five hours, they carry out the last family's furniture and carry in yours. By the time you come back from the parade, your toothbrush is waiting for you.

And what a house. A hundred and thirty-two rooms. Thirty-five bathrooms. A bowling alley in the basement, a movie theater, a tennis court, and a chef who has cooked for presidents before you.

One surprise, though. The food isn't free. Official dinners are paid for by the government, but your family's groceries, your snacks, your toothpaste and your dry cleaning all show up on a bill at the end of every month. You do get paid. The salary is four hundred thousand dollars a year. It sounds like a lot, until you remember you're running a country with more than three hundred million people in it.

So what does a normal day look like?

It starts early. Before breakfast, someone hands you the President's Daily Brief. It's a short, top-secret summary of everything the intelligence agencies think you need to know: a missile test overnight, a coup in a country you've barely heard of, a hacker group poking at power plants. Some mornings it's dull. Some mornings it changes the whole day.

Then you walk to work. It's a short walk, through the colonnade, past the Rose Garden, into the Oval Office. You sit behind the Resolute Desk, built from the timbers of a British ship that was trapped in Arctic ice.

From there, your day is cut into fifteen-minute pieces. A meeting on the economy. A call with a prime minister. A photo with a Little League team. A budget fight with Congress. Lunch, if you're lucky, at your desk. Your schedule is planned weeks ahead, and somebody else holds it.

And everywhere you go, a few people go with you.

The Secret Service is always there. Agents stand outside your bedroom door at night. They walk ahead of you into every room. They decide which doors you use and which windows you stand near. You'll get a code name. You won't be allowed to drive a car on a public road again, possibly for the rest of your life.

One more person follows you everywhere: a military aide carrying a black leather bag. People call it the football. Inside are the plans and the codes for a nuclear launch. It's in the next room when you sleep, on the plane when you fly, and at the back of the restaurant if you ever manage to go out to dinner. It's the heaviest thing you'll ever carry, and you never actually touch it.

Now, the decisions.

Nobody tells you this part of the job. The easy problems never reach your desk. If a question has an obvious answer, someone below you already answered it. What lands in front of you are the ones where every option is bad, and you're picking the least bad.

Do you send troops, or don't you? Do you sign a bill you half agree with, or veto it and start over? When Congress sends you a bill, you have ten days, not counting Sundays, to sign it or send it back. If you veto it, they can still override you, but they need two-thirds of both houses to do it.

Some decisions happen in a windowless room in the basement of the West Wing called the Situation Room. It's smaller than you'd expect. The screens are big, the coffee is bad, and the people around the table are waiting for you to say yes or no.

And whatever you decide, around half the country will think you got it wrong. Some days it'll be more than half.

So how do you stay connected to real life?

One way is the mail. The White House gets thousands of letters a day. A team reads them, and each night a small stack lands on your desk. Barack Obama read ten of them every evening. People write about lost jobs, sick kids, a son coming home from war. Some of them thank you. Plenty of them tell you, in detail, how badly you're doing. Those letters are often the most honest part of the day.

Then there are the strange traditions. Every November, you pardon a turkey in the Rose Garden. You throw the first pitch at baseball games and hope it reaches the plate. You host an Easter egg roll on the South Lawn for thousands of kids. You light the national Christmas tree. None of it is in the Constitution. All of it is expected.

Travel is its own world. When you fly, the plane is called Air Force One. It has an office, a medical room, a conference room and a bedroom. Helicopters called Marine One carry you to and from the lawn, usually flying in groups so nobody on the ground knows which one you're in. A trip to a single city can take hundreds of people, cargo planes full of armored cars, and a motorcade that shuts down highways for miles.

On weekends, if things are quiet, you might go to Camp David, a guarded retreat in the Maryland woods. Things are rarely quiet.

And then there's what the job does to the person.

Look at photos of any president on their first day, and then on their last. The hair goes grey. The lines get deeper. You'll sleep less. Your kids grow up with agents at their school. Your friends start to talk to you a little differently. Every word you say is recorded, replayed and picked apart. A joke at the wrong moment becomes a headline.

But you also get moments almost no one else gets. You welcome soldiers home. You stand with a town the morning after a tornado. You sign a law that changes how millions of people live, and you hand the pen to someone who spent twenty years fighting for it.

Then, just like that, it ends.

Since 1951, the Twenty-Second Amendment has limited presidents to two terms. Four years, maybe eight, and it's over. On your final morning, you leave a handwritten letter in the desk drawer for the next person. You walk out the same door you came in. The movers are already waiting.

After that, you'll get a pension, an office, and Secret Service protection for life. People will still call you Mr. or Madam President. You'll write a book, build a library, and watch someone else make the calls you used to make.

So, would you want it?

Before you answer, remember the morning of January 20th. At 11:59, you could go anywhere, eat anything, and say whatever you wanted. At noon, the whole country becomes your responsibility. Every president who's ever lived made that trade. Very few of them would tell you it was easy. Almost none of them would tell you they regret it.
